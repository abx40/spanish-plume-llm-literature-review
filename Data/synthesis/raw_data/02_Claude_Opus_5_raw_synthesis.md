# Critical Cross-Paper Synthesis: The Spanish Plume in the Closed Corpus SP001–SP102

**Scope and rules.** This synthesis uses only the 102 PDFs in `./sources`. Page
citations are `[SPxxx, PDF p. N]`, where N is the one-based page position within
the PDF file, not the printed journal page. Every analytical paragraph carries
exactly one evidence-status label. Schultz, Young and Kirshbaum (2025) is absent
from the corpus and is not used; no prior model output, external database, or
web source was consulted.

**A note on extraction artefacts that affect attribution.** Several PDFs in this
corpus are magazine pages that carry column fragments of *neighbouring* articles.
The most consequential are SP006, whose PDF page 12 reproduces the opening of
Young (1995) — which is separately catalogued as SP005 — and SP038, whose PDF
page 1 reproduces the closing column of Sibley (2012), separately catalogued as
SP034. Text on those pages is not attributed to the host source anywhere below.

## 1. Evolution of the term and concept

**Direct finding** — The earliest use of the phrase in this corpus is Carlson and
Ludlam (1968), where "Spanish plume" names a physical body of air rather than a
weather pattern. Their abstract locates extreme instability where small-scale
convection is confined to the lowest 1–2 km "beneath a plume of very warm air
lying downwind of an extensive arid plateau" (Spain or Mexico), and states that
"The instability is released where the (backed) low-level flow eventually reaches
the edge of the restraining plume aloft" [SP001, PDF p. 1]. In the case analyses
the term is used concretely — castellanus form "near or west of the edge of the
Spanish plume" [SP001, PDF p. 8], and air in the French adiabatic layer moves
"beneath the Spanish plume" [SP001, PDF p. 9].

**Direct finding** — Salter (1968), published the same year, applies the phrase to
a mapped field: an area of potentially very warm air at 850 hPa and, less so, at
700 hPa over parts of Spain and France, described as "this 'Spanish plume'" with
a superscript reference marker [SP002, PDF p. 6]. Salter's numbered reference list
contains Carlson and Ludlam (1968) as its second entry [SP002, PDF p. 9], so on
the ordering of that list the marker points to Carlson and Ludlam; the numerals
and the entries are set in separate columns, so this is read from layout order
rather than from an explicit pairing. Salter's mechanism is in any case the same as
Carlson and Ludlam's: convection in France "was minimal because there was a plume
of potentially warm air which effectively acted like a lid", released by "the
escape of the high surface WBPT from beneath the plume of warm air" [SP002,
PDF p. 6].

**Direct finding** — Morris (1986) changes what the term denotes. The paper's
section "Mechanics of the development" lists "The main conditions for development
of a Spanish plume": an upper trough slowly advancing towards Biscay and Iberia,
and a zone of strong low-tropospheric temperature gradient over Iberia [SP003,
PDF p. 2]. It then narrates a synoptic evolution culminating in the clause "the
Spanish plume is born" [SP003, PDF p. 2], and describes the whole development as
a weather pattern that "usually occurs at least once or twice during the summer
months between May and September" and "is known as the 'Spanish plume'" [SP003,
PDF p. 1]. The object named is now a synoptic-scale development, not a layer of
air.

**Direct finding** — Subsequent UK operational literature adopts Morris's usage and
labels it a conceptual model. McCallum and Waters (1993) call the situation
"known colloquially as the 'Spanish plume'" [SP004, PDF p. 2] and close by
crediting "the 'Spanish plume concept'" as a useful newer conceptual model
[SP004, PDF p. 11]. Young (1995) states that the synoptic pattern "conformed very
well with the 'Spanish plume' conceptual model (Morris 1986)" [SP005, PDF p. 1].
Gray and Marshall (1998) define the pattern by three components — cold advection
into Iberia, low-level warm advection over northern France and/or southern
England, and an eastward-propagating upper trough west of the UK [SP016,
PDF p. 7].

**Direct finding** — Lewis and Gray (2010) formalise the concept into a named
taxonomy. Their abstract identifies "three distinct synoptic environments … here
termed the classical Spanish plume, modified Spanish plume, and European easterly
plume" [SP031, PDF p. 1]. Of 25 UK mesoscale convective systems (MCSs) between
1998 and 2008, nine were classified classical, ten modified and five European
easterly, with one unclassified [SP031, PDF p. 19]. The paper is explicit that
this is "subjective categorisation" and "a necessary pre-requisite to future
objective analyses" [SP031, PDF p. 4].

**Direct finding** — Two sources in the corpus give an explicit historical account
of the term's adoption, and they agree with each other. Young and Grahame state
that the term "was almost certainly first used by Carlson and Ludlam (1968)
before being resurrected by Morris (1986) after which it was widely adopted by
forecasters" [SP097, PDF p. 5], and, in the companion paper, that "the first
reference to the term 'Spanish Plume' was almost certainly in Carlson and Ludlam
(1968)", not adopted into regular operational use until after Morris (1986)
[SP098, PDF p. 4].

**Direct finding** — Two other attributions in the corpus differ from that account.
A third paper in the same series attributes the thundery 'Spanish plume' to
"(Salter, 1968)" [SP099, PDF p. 3]. Separately, Field (1999) argues that
observations by Douglas and Harding (1946) "contain a pre-science of the
phenomenon known as the 'Spanish plume'" [SP018, PDF p. 6], placing an
unnamed antecedent two decades before the term itself.

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — Across the corpus the concept passes through
four stages: (i) a diagnosed air mass with a stated release mechanism [SP001,
PDF p. 1; SP002, PDF p. 6]; (ii) a synoptic development defined by its precursors
and forecastable from NWP output [SP003, PDF pp. 1–2; SP004, PDF p. 9; SP005,
PDF p. 1]; (iii) a classified family of synoptic environments with quantified
membership [SP016, PDF p. 7; SP031, PDF pp. 1, 19]; and (iv) a label attached to
objectively derived weather-pattern classes [SP083, PDF pp. 11, 20; SP090,
PDF p. 17; SP102, PDF p. 16]. The sources disagree about the founding reference —
SP097 and SP098 name Carlson and Ludlam, SP099 names Salter, SP018 identifies an
unnamed 1946 antecedent — and the corpus contains no source that adjudicates
between them.

## 2. Airstream versus synoptic-pattern usage

**Direct finding** — One source in the corpus states the ambiguity explicitly. Dahl
and Fischer (2016) note that over Europe the main EML source is the Iberian
Peninsula and "the respective EML is sometimes referred to as a Spanish plume
(Carlson and Ludlam 1968; Bennett et al. 2006)", but that more recently "the
overall synoptic pattern involving an EML (rather than the EML itself)" has been
called a Spanish plume, citing Morris (1986) and Lewis and Gray (2010) [SP056,
PDF p. 4]. This is the only place in the corpus where the two senses are
distinguished and both are named.

**Direct finding** — The airstream sense is in continuous use. Van Delden writes
that "the Spanish plume acts as a capping inversion preventing deep convection"
and that potential instability is created "Above the Spanish plume" [SP014,
PDF p. 2; see also SP019, PDF p. 13]. Peyraud's figure caption identifies "the
dry elevated mixed layer (Spanish plume)" [SP040, PDF p. 2]. De Meutter and
Gerard define it as "an air mass originating from the Spanish Plateau"
characterised by steep mid-tropospheric lapse rates and dry air near 850 hPa
[SP050, PDF p. 6]. Piper and Kunz refer to "Elevated mixed layers originating
from the Spanish plateau, known as the Spanish Plume" [SP062, PDF p. 5], and
elsewhere to "an elevated mixed layer (EML), known as the Spanish Plume over
western France" [SP073, PDF p. 15]. Young et al. write of "'Spanish Plume'
(elevated mixed layer) convection" [SP078, PDF p. 10].

**Direct finding** — The synoptic-pattern sense is equally entrenched. Hamid calls
it a "synoptic setup" and "a configuration" [SP035, PDF p. 1]; Wilhelm et al. the
"Spanish plume pattern" [SP084, PDF p. 3]; Feldmann and Germann "a particularly
potent synoptic constellation" [SP087, PDF p. 7]; Winter and Galvin describe an
event that "can generally be described as a 'Spanish Plume' event" [SP082,
PDF p. 1]. In objective classifications the term becomes a label for a
circulation class: ERA5 weather patterns 16 and 22 "are linked to 'Spanish
Plumes'" [SP083, PDF p. 20], pattern 16 is called "the Spanish Plume weather
pattern" [SP102, PDF p. 16], and a lightning climatology reports flash counts
"under weather pattern 16 (Spanish Plume)" [SP090, PDF p. 19].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — The two usages are not merely different levels
of description; in the corpus they attach the same name to opposite air masses.
In the founding usage the plume is the *warm, dry, elevated* layer that caps
convection: instability builds "beneath a plume of very warm air" [SP001,
PDF p. 1], and the plume "acts as a capping inversion" [SP014, PDF p. 2], is "the
dry elevated mixed layer" [SP040, PDF p. 2], and is "warm, dry air from the
Iberian plateau (the Spanish plume)" [SP070, PDF p. 2]. In the most-cited modern
classification the plume is instead the *moist, low-level* air beneath the cap:
"Conditional instability is released from a warm moist plume of air advected
northeastwards from Iberia that is capped by warmer, but very dry air, from the
Spanish plateau" [SP031, PDF p. 1], a reading reproduced downstream as "warm moist
air from Iberia capped by warmer but very dry air from the Spanish plateau"
[SP083, PDF p. 11] and as warm moist air brought "up from Iberia" [SP102,
PDF p. 16]. No source in the corpus reconciles these two referents, and SP056
[PDF p. 4] records only the airstream/pattern distinction, not this inversion.

**Author interpretation** — Some sources use "plume" for both the capping layer and
the moist inflow within a single argument without marking the shift. Holley et al.
describe CAPE maxima "during Spanish plume events" and separately track "plume
axis orientation" and plume "tracks" visible over cool seas [SP046, PDF pp. 1, 5,
6], where the mapped quantity is boundary-layer CAPE rather than the elevated
layer. This is the authors' own framing, not a measured property of the plume.

## 3. Thermodynamic and moisture properties

**Direct finding** — The corpus agrees on a two-layer thermodynamic structure: a
moist boundary layer beneath a warm, dry, well-mixed elevated layer with steep
lapse rates, separated by an inversion. Peyraud's schematic sounding shows "moist
low levels overridden by the dry elevated mixed layer (Spanish plume) and steep
midlevel lapse rates" [SP040, PDF p. 2]. De Meutter and Gerard record dry air
between 900 and 800 hPa and a steep lapse rate from 900 to 300 hPa in the Trappes
sounding [SP050, PDF p. 6]. Mathias et al. identify the EML between 800 and
650 hPa at Bergen-Hohne [SP063, PDF p. 6]. Scheffknecht et al. resolve a weakly
stable, relatively moist elevated mixed layer above 700 hPa in three profiles
[SP058, PDF p. 9].

**Direct finding** — Operational practice in the UK sources identifies the plume by
850 hPa wet-bulb potential temperature thresholds, but the thresholds differ.
McCallum and Waters mark "the 'Spanish plume'" by the area of θw > 18 °C [SP004,
PDF p. 3]; Young highlights the 16 °C isopleth "to emphasise the Spanish plume"
[SP005, PDF p. 2]; Galvin et al. report 850 hPa θw greater than 17 °C [SP006,
PDF p. 4]; Young and Grahame use a tongue of WBPT > 18 °C [SP097, PDF p. 6].
Young also notes a warm 'nose' near 800 hPa "being characteristic of the Spanish
plume", with the inversion immediately below it expected to prevent early
surface-based convection [SP005, PDF p. 2]; Elsom et al. describe "a conspicuous
warm 'nose' between 850 and 700 hPa (typical of the 'Spanish Plume')" [SP066,
PDF p. 8].

**Direct finding** — Where the source-region contrast is quantified, it comes from
Carlson and Ludlam via Bennett et al.: the afternoon screen-level θ over the
Spanish plateau is typically about 36 °C in summer against 26–28 °C over the
moister lower ground of France [SP026, PDF p. 12]. Carlson and Ludlam themselves
report screen-level θ over central Spain reaching a little over 37 °C [SP001,
PDF p. 8] and note that over southern France surface θw reached 24 °C, a value
otherwise found on their section only south of the intertropical front over West
Africa [SP001, PDF p. 2]. Bennett et al. add that mean θw in a 1–2 km mixed layer
over southern France increases by about 2.5 degC in 24 hours under the lid
[SP026, PDF p. 12].

**Direct finding** — Reported CAPE values associated with plume events span an order
of magnitude. Holley et al. simulate the highest CAPE of their ten-year British
climatology on 5–6 August 2003, locally exceeding 3000 J kg⁻¹ over north Wales
during a Spanish plume [SP046, PDF p. 5]. Steeneveld and Peerlings give ~3 × 10³
J kg⁻¹ observed and cite ~2.5 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ as typical for MCSs and Spanish plumes
in western Europe [SP077, PDF pp. 3, 10]. Mathias et al. report 2000–3000 J kg⁻¹
with significant CIN [SP063, PDF p. 6]. Lewis et al. report 2000 J kg⁻¹ above
1800 m in an AMDAR profile [SP061, PDF p. 5]. By contrast, an aviation
verification scheme treats "approximately greater than 350 J/kg" as the high-impact
threshold and cites Spanish plume events as an example [SP070, PDF p. 2].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — There is no agreed quantitative definition of
the plume's thermodynamic signature in this corpus. The θw thresholds used to
draw the plume on a chart vary between 16 and 18 °C across four UK operational
sources [SP004, PDF p. 3; SP005, PDF p. 2; SP006, PDF p. 4; SP097, PDF p. 6];
the pressure interval assigned to the elevated layer varies from 900–700 hPa
[SP014, PDF p. 2] through 800–650 hPa [SP063, PDF p. 6] to a general 700–500 hPa
[SP057, PDF p. 18] and to 825–500 hPa in one UK case [SP061, PDF p. 2]; and the
CAPE magnitudes treated as diagnostic differ by nearly an order of magnitude
between research and aviation-operational sources [SP046, PDF p. 5; SP070,
PDF p. 2]. Holley et al. explicitly warn against reading CAPE as sufficient:
high CAPE on 6 August 2003 produced no further thunderstorms because of high CIN
and the clearance of forcing [SP046, PDF p. 5].

## 4. Claimed geographical origin and transport pathways

**Direct finding** — The dominant claim is an Iberian source. Carlson and Ludlam
place the plume downwind of "an extensive arid plateau (Spain or Mexico)" [SP001,
PDF p. 1]. Bennett et al. state that "The warm lid of the Spanish Plume originates
from a relatively deep mixed boundary layer generated over the arid Spanish
plateau" [SP026, PDF p. 12]. Agustí-Panareda et al. define the events as a tongue
of warm relatively dry low-level air advected northward from the Spanish plateau
ahead of an eastward-propagating upper trough [SP030, PDF p. 1; SP024, PDF p. 2].
Dahl and Fischer state that over Europe the main EML source is the Iberian
Peninsula [SP056, PDF p. 4]. Further Iberian attributions appear in [SP035,
PDF p. 1], [SP050, PDF p. 6], [SP062, PDF p. 5], [SP063, PDF p. 6], [SP064,
PDF p. 9], [SP070, PDF p. 2], [SP073, PDF p. 15], [SP077, PDF pp. 1, 3], [SP082,
PDF p. 1], [SP084, PDF p. 3] and [SP102, PDF p. 16].

**Direct finding** — A substantial minority of sources claim a North African source,
either instead of or alongside Iberia. Dahl and Fischer's own result concerns an
EML plume "generated over the Iberian Peninsula and northern Africa" [SP056,
PDF p. 1], and their conceptual model has a deep convective boundary layer present
over the Atlas Mountains as well as Iberia [SP056, PDF p. 13]; they treat the
northern Sahara as a separate, named EML source (the Saharan air layer) [SP056,
PDF p. 4]. Punge and Kunz state that the air mass "has been formed over NW Africa
or on the Central Iberian Plateau" [SP057, PDF p. 18]. Taszarek et al. list
European EML development as "confined to the Iberian Peninsula, the Alps, Turkey,
and northwestern Africa" [SP081, PDF p. 8]. Scheffknecht et al., analysing an
Alpine supercell, judge that relative to the Spanish plume scenario "warm air
appears to be advected horizontally from North Africa rather than from Spain"
[SP058, PDF p. 4]. Sibley states that the very warm air moves northwards "across
the Spanish Plateau from Africa" [SP034, PDF p. 1], and Sibley and Cox describe
storms "moving northwards from Africa and Spain" [SP086, PDF p. 5].

**Direct finding** — Two sources place moisture or heat sources elsewhere again.
Salter found the abnormally high surface WBPT under the plume "to have been
advected from the Mediterranean" [SP002, PDF p. 6]; Peyraud's Swiss case has
low-level moisture from the Mediterranean beneath the Iberian mid-level layer
[SP040, PDF p. 5]; Bissolli et al. attribute the warm humid air to advection
"from the Atlantic and the Mediterranean" [SP029, PDF p. 13]. Archer and Fowler
describe the rain-bearing air as "a tongue of moist air extending from the
Tropics — often called a 'Spanish Plume' or 'Atmospheric River'" [SP067,
PDF p. 6], and Lewis et al. describe Spanish plumes as "bringing warm air
polewards from the Equator" [SP100, PDF p. 12].

**Direct finding** — The corpus reports several distinct transport pathways. The
canonical one is Iberia → Bay of Biscay → France → southern England [SP001,
PDF p. 9; SP003, PDF p. 1; SP031, PDF p. 19]. Lewis and Gray's Table 2 assigns
different low-level plume origins and propagation directions to each of their
three environments: Iberia with propagation northeast across southeast England
(classical), Iberia with propagation north across western and northern England
(modified), and northern continental Europe with propagation northwest across
southern England (European easterly) [SP031, PDF p. 19]. Other pathways in the
corpus run Iberia → Pyrenees → the Netherlands [SP077, PDF p. 1], Iberia → France
→ Belgium and Germany [SP035, PDF p. 3; SP052, PDF p. 3], and Algerian Sahara →
western Mediterranean → Alps → central and eastern Europe [SP084, PDF p. 4].

**Direct finding** — At least one source keeps Iberian and Saharan plumes as
separate categories in the same classification. Berthet et al. tabulate mesoscale
patterns for southwestern French hail days that include both "Saharan plume" and
"Spanish plume" as distinct entries [SP039, PDF p. 9].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — Origin claims in the corpus fall into three
tiers of specificity that are frequently conflated: the *elevated mixed layer's*
source region (Iberian meseta, Atlas/northern Sahara, Alps, Turkey) [SP056,
PDF p. 4; SP081, PDF p. 8; SP057, PDF p. 18]; the *low-level moisture's* source
(Mediterranean, Atlantic, Bay of Biscay) [SP002, PDF p. 6; SP040, PDF p. 5;
SP029, PDF p. 13]; and a loose *airmass provenance* stated without layer
attribution ("from Africa", "from the Tropics", "polewards from the Equator")
[SP034, PDF p. 1; SP067, PDF p. 6; SP100, PDF p. 12]. Sources that analyse the
layers separately consistently separate the two source regions [SP056, PDF p. 13;
SP040, PDF p. 5; SP063, PDF p. 6]; sources that assert a single origin for the
whole system are typically short case reports or passing mentions [SP034,
PDF p. 1; SP067, PDF p. 6; SP100, PDF p. 12].

## 5. Evidence used to support origin claims

**Direct finding** — The founding origin claim rests on isentropic relative-flow
analysis and radiosonde soundings. Carlson and Ludlam state their technique
explicitly — analyses on isentropic surfaces under a steady-state assumption,
where "the streamlines of the flow relative to the system are trajectories"
[SP001, PDF p. 3] — and use it to show air from the Spanish adiabatic layer
present between 850 and 700 hPa with the lid at 850 hPa [SP001, PDF p. 7], and to
trace flow from the French adiabatic layer beneath the plume's western boundary
[SP001, PDF p. 9]. Their meridional sections are built from named radiosonde
stations spanning Lagos to Sodankylä, with the Atlas mountains and Spain shown
schematically [SP001, PDF p. 2].

**Direct finding** — Three sources in the corpus support origin claims with explicit
trajectory computations, and they do not all point to Iberia. Lewis et al. report
an air mass that "originated from the elevated Spanish Plateau 2 days previously",
with backward trajectories also identifying an additional shallow warm moist layer
near 850 hPa [SP061, PDF p. 2]. De Villiers finds trajectories that "developed
into a Spanish Plume pattern on the 25th … originating from North Africa west of
Algiers, or the south of Spain" [SP076, PDF p. 5]. Wilhelm et al. report that
"an EML plume from the Algerian Sahara crossed the western Mediterranean and the
Alps to reach central and eastern Europe, as model backward trajectories suggest",
with the trajectories themselves not shown [SP084, PDF p. 4].

**Direct finding** — Two sources use tracer transport experiments in a
mesoscale model. Agustí-Panareda et al. attribute localised regions of young
tracer in northeast Europe to MCSs "triggered within the Spanish plume" [SP024,
PDF p. 6] and characterise their case as one where the resolved prefrontal ascent
is weak but the low- and mid-level θe plume creates large CAPE [SP024, PDF p. 12];
the companion study extends this to a comparison across frontal types [SP030,
PDF p. 1].

**Direct finding** — Model-based and composite evidence dominates the later
literature. Dahl and Fischer combine WRF simulations with Q-vector diagnostics and
a three-year composite analysis of cold fronts with and without preceding
convergence lines using NCEP–NCAR Reanalysis-1 [SP056, PDF p. 1]. Lewis and Gray
build composites of mean sea level pressure, 500 hPa height and 300/200 hPa winds
from 40-year NCEP/NCAR reanalysis, noting that "Compositing smoothes the detailed,
case dependent features of the fields and is less appropriate for small numbers of
cases" and that consistency checks on individual days were therefore made [SP031,
PDF p. 5]. Wilkinson and Neal work from a 30-class ERA5 weather-pattern set
[SP083, PDF p. 11].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — Weighting analytical studies above passing
mentions, the evidential support for the Iberian-origin claim is strong but
narrower than its ubiquity suggests. Only one corpus source computes trajectories
supporting an Iberian plateau origin, and that for a single UK case [SP061,
PDF p. 2]; two compute trajectories supporting a North African origin, also for
single cases, one of them not shown [SP076, PDF p. 5; SP084, PDF p. 4]. The
remaining Iberian attributions rest either on the original isentropic analysis
[SP001, PDF pp. 3, 7, 9], on model θ/θw fields at a single level [SP003, PDF p. 3;
SP004, PDF p. 3; SP005, PDF p. 2], on sounding structure consistent with an EML
[SP050, PDF p. 6; SP063, PDF p. 6], or on citation of earlier work. Sources that
merely cite predecessors for the origin claim — for example [SP029, PDF p. 13],
[SP032, PDF p. 10], [SP069, PDF p. 9], [SP080, PDF p. 15], [SP094, PDF p. 8] —
add no independent evidence, and their repetition should not be read as
corroboration.

## 6. Capping, instability, and convection-initiation mechanisms

**Direct finding** — The corpus agrees that the elevated warm layer suppresses
convection and permits energy to accumulate. Salter states that convection in
France was minimal because the plume "effectively acted like a lid" [SP002,
PDF p. 6]. Bennett et al. state that "The restraining lid described above plays a
crucial role in allowing convective available potential energy (CAPE) to build
up", and that a stronger lid permits larger CAPE but requires more lifting to
release it "so long as differential advection does not allow the low-level air to
reach the horizontal boundary of the lid" [SP026, PDF p. 11]. Gray and Marshall
state that cold advection aloft provides large CAPE that cannot be released
spontaneously because warm air from the Spanish plateau creates a stable layer
around 900 hPa [SP016, PDF p. 7]. Mathias et al. record that the EML capped
2000–3000 J kg⁻¹ of CAPE and produced significant CIN, "also often referred to
the so-called loaded-gun scenario" [SP063, PDF p. 6]; van Delden uses the same
American term for the state above the plume [SP014, PDF p. 2; SP019, PDF p. 13].

**Direct finding** — The corpus contains at least six distinct proposed release
mechanisms, attributed to different authors. (i) *Edge-of-lid escape*: instability
is released where the backed low-level flow reaches the western edge of the dry
plume aloft, that edge being marked by a cold front [SP001, PDF p. 1; SP026,
PDF p. 13]. (ii) *Quasi-geostrophic ascent ahead of the upper trough*: dynamical
ascent from vorticity and thermal advection, deduced via the omega equation
[SP003, PDF p. 2], with warm advection and upper-level positive vorticity
advection eventually allowing CAPE release [SP031, PDF p. 2]. (iii)
*Frontogenesis and the cross-frontal circulation*: warm advection intensifies the
low-level cold front, and readjustment to thermal wind balance produces upward
motion at the front's leading edge, which van Delden identifies as decisive for
triggering the storms [SP014, PDF p. 17]; potential instability up to great
heights is created by the cross-frontal circulation in the warm-advection region
[SP014, PDF p. 16]. (The concluding section of SP014 is set with letter-spacing
that the PDF text layer does not reproduce as ordinary words, so this mechanism is
paraphrased rather than quoted.)
(iv) *Prefrontal convergence lines*: Dahl and Fischer show the lines are
associated with the 850 hPa thermal ridge at the base of the EML plume, with the
ascending branch of the resulting ageostrophic circulation coinciding with surface
velocity convergence [SP056, PDF pp. 1, 12]. (v) *Orographic and thermally driven
lift*: convection initiated where dry downslope flow meets moist air, and by
plain-to-mountain and upslope flows beneath the EML [SP058, PDF pp. 4, 12], and
adiabatic warming from foehn-like conditions steepening lapse rates [SP084,
PDF p. 4]. (vi) *Short-wave troughs embedded in the flow*: destabilisation of the
plume by a shortwave on 5 August 2003 [SP046, PDF p. 5], and focused ascent from
embedded shortwaves that was insufficient until evening in one northern England
case [SP061, PDF p. 2].

**Direct finding** — Elevated, non-surface-based initiation is repeatedly identified
as both important and poorly understood. Young and Grahame state that overnight
Spanish Plume developments "remained particularly problematic, given that they
would be exclusively dominated by convection triggered well above the surface by
processes that were poorly understood and remain so to this day" [SP097,
PDF p. 6]. Lewis and Gray note that surface-based convection advected across the
cold English Channel in late spring is inhibited by stable boundary-layer
stratification, so that events crossing it must release instability from elevated
unstable layers, which requires strong mid-level shear for organisation and is
therefore infrequent [SP031, PDF p. 6]. Elsom et al. describe an episode in which
"Extensive mid-level thunderstorm activity occurred during the evening and night
… as the elevated plume was destabilised" [SP066, PDF p. 8].

**Author interpretation** — Wind shear is treated throughout as the organisational
ingredient rather than a trigger. Carlson and Ludlam place the storms near
confluence lines "where an increase of wind with height favours the organisation
and intensification of cumulonimbus convection" [SP001, PDF p. 1]; Lewis and Gray
state that strong vertical shear near the front enables organisation of the
thunderstorms into an MCS [SP031, PDF p. 2]; Bennett et al. attribute updraught–
downdraught separation to directional shear [SP026, PDF p. 14]. These are the
authors' interpretations of case and composite evidence, not separately measured
sensitivities.

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — The corpus offers no test that discriminates
between the six release mechanisms above, and the sources that propose them use
different diagnostic frameworks — isentropic relative-flow analysis [SP001,
PDF p. 3], the quasi-geostrophic omega equation [SP003, PDF p. 2], a frontal
intensity equation [SP014, PDF p. 1], and Q-vector decomposition [SP056,
PDF p. 1]. Dahl and Fischer state that the dynamical origin of the western
European convergence lines "has barely been addressed in the formal literature
despite their ubiquity" [SP056, PDF p. 1], and note that van Delden and Kaltenböck
had proposed competing explanations (frontal intensification versus differential
diabatic heating) [SP056, PDF p. 1]. On the evidence in this corpus the
mechanisms are best read as complementary and case-dependent rather than as
competing general theories; no source demonstrates which dominates in a
climatological sense.

## 7. Hazards, locations, and event types

**Direct finding** — The most quantified association is with mesoscale convective
systems over the UK. Gray and Marshall report that "Of the 32 MCSs studied, 25 are
associated with a Spanish plume", with two more probable but unconfirmed for lack
of data [SP016, PDF p. 7]. Lewis and Gray extend the climatology to 1998–2008,
finding 25 events at an annual average of 2.3 per year against Gray and Marshall's
1.8, with no obvious trend across the combined 28-year record, and with about 70%
of events advected over the UK from the Bay of Biscay or France rather than
initiated locally [SP031, PDF pp. 5, 18]. Chagnon and Gray give the same
approximate frequency of two per year [SP028, PDF p. 6].

**Direct finding** — Reported hazards span hail, tornadoes, flash flooding,
lightning, damaging wind and coastal waves. Hail: an early-season event with
"giant" hail [SP013, PDF p. 1]; a severe English Midlands hailstorm in the
"classic" scenario [SP042, PDF p. 1]; 3–5 cm hail in Germany with EML advection
[SP084, PDF pp. 4, 6]; hail 50 mm or more on some 16 UK occasions since the 1940s,
nearly all with features of the scenario [SP096, PDF p. 3]. Tornadoes: German
tornado weather types linked to the pattern [SP029, PDF p. 13]; a tornadic
supercell with a microburst gust of 40 m s⁻¹ and material damage over $100 million
in a Spanish plume configuration [SP040, PDF p. 24]; violent European tornado
environments typically associated with the setup [SP093, PDF p. 4]. Damaging wind:
an asymmetric trailing-stratiform QLCS with bowing segments crossing France,
Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands [SP035, PDF p. 2]; a bow echo requiring the
plume-induced cap to be overcome [SP063, PDF p. 9]; ~€675 million of damage from
an MCS "formed by a Spanish plume" [SP077, PDF p. 1]. Lightning: exceptional
counts on 28 June 2012 attributed to "a classic 'Spanish plume'" [SP066,
PDF p. 7]; patterns 16 and 22 producing lightning "more intense than is observed
in any other season or weather pattern" [SP083, PDF p. 20].

**Direct finding** — Meteotsunami is a distinct hazard class linked to the pattern
in four corpus sources. Sibley and Cox attribute a meteotsunami along English
Channel and North Sea coasts to elevated convective storm conditions "as part of a
'Spanish Plume'" [SP086, PDF p. 11]. Williams et al. find their quasi-linear
summertime synoptic composite "broadly consistent with a Spanish Plume pattern"
[SP088, PDF p. 14]. Sibley describes a June 2022 case that "can be thought of as a
modified 'Spanish Plume' event" [SP091, PDF p. 2]. Lewis et al. report a 71%
positive correlation between summer meteotsunami events and convective systems
that "can occur within synoptic Spanish plume settings" [SP100, PDF p. 13].

**Direct finding** — Impacts on aviation and public safety are documented but the
lightning-fatality link is weak. Brown and Buchanan treat plume events as
high-impact for UK air traffic, with heavy flow-rate regulation expected [SP070,
PDF p. 2]; two historical accounts describe unforecast plume cyclogenesis
disrupting Heathrow operations [SP089, PDF p. 2; SP098, PDF p. 4]. Against this,
Elsom and Webb find that of 19 UK days on which leisure walkers were struck and
killed by lightning in 1987–2016, "Only four of the days occurred in the classic
or modified Spanish Plume situation" [SP060, PDF p. 12], with the remaining 15
distributed across three other synoptic scenarios [SP060, PDF p. 13].

**Direct finding** — The geographical focus is southeast England in the UK sources
but the pattern is invoked across much of Europe. Lewis and Gray state that the
classical environment is favourable for MCSs over southeast England, with
mid-level steering typically southwesterly [SP031, PDF p. 16]. Beyond the UK the
corpus applies the concept to Switzerland [SP040, PDF p. 5], Germany [SP065,
PDF p. 2; SP084, PDF p. 3], Belgium [SP035, PDF p. 1; SP052, PDF p. 3], the
Netherlands [SP077, PDF p. 1], France [SP101, PDF p. 7], the Alps [SP058,
PDF p. 4; SP087, PDF p. 7], Poland [SP095, PDF p. 14], and the Mediterranean
basin by analogy [SP041, PDF p. 11; SP008, PDF p. 3]. Finnish and Polish
Carpathian studies describe their own MCS environments as having "some
similarities to the Spanish plume" or as reminiscent of it, without claiming
identity [SP023, PDF p. 1; SP068, PDF p. 12; SP074, PDF p. 15]; one Czech source
posits an analogous "South France plume" [SP009, PDF p. 8].

**Direct finding** — Seasonality is consistently warm-season. Morris gives May to
September [SP003, PDF p. 1]; Lewis and Gray find no significant pattern within
June–August, a significant number of late-May events and two in mid-September, and
attribute the absence of autumn, winter and spring MCSs to the lack of the high
boundary-layer θw required for deep convection [SP031, PDF p. 6]; Dahl and Fischer
argue the cold-season absence follows from the low solar elevation angle failing
to build a deep convective boundary layer over Iberia, with the northern Saharan
source not easily tapped because cyclone tracks lie farther north in winter
[SP056, PDF p. 12]. Bennett et al. report a strong diurnal cycle, with 50% of
MCSs beginning between 1800 and 0000 UTC and 25% between 0000 and 0600 UTC
[SP026, PDF p. 14].

## 8. Areas of agreement

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — Six propositions are supported by multiple
independent analytical studies in the corpus and contradicted by none of them.
First, a deep, dry, well-mixed layer generated over hot elevated arid terrain can
be advected off that terrain over cooler, moister air, forming an elevated mixed
layer with a capping inversion [SP001, PDF p. 1; SP026, PDF pp. 11–12; SP056,
PDF p. 4; SP057, PDF p. 18; SP081, PDF p. 8]. Second, that cap suppresses
surface-based convection and permits large CAPE and large CIN to coexist [SP002,
PDF p. 6; SP016, PDF p. 7; SP026, PDF p. 11; SP063, PDF p. 6; SP084, PDF p. 6].
Third, the European instance is associated with an amplifying upper trough
approaching Biscay and Iberia from the Atlantic and with low-level warm advection
ahead of it [SP003, PDF p. 2; SP004, PDF p. 3; SP016, PDF p. 7; SP031,
PDF pp. 2, 16]. Fourth, release of the instability requires an identifiable lifting
agent and does not follow from high CAPE alone [SP026, PDF p. 11; SP031, PDF p. 2;
SP046, PDF p. 5; SP063, PDF p. 9]. Fifth, the resulting convection is frequently
organised into MCSs and is often nocturnal and advected across the UK from the
continent [SP016, PDF p. 7; SP026, PDF p. 14; SP031, PDF pp. 6, 18; SP090,
PDF p. 14]. Sixth, the configuration is a warm-season phenomenon [SP003, PDF p. 1;
SP031, PDF p. 6; SP056, PDF p. 12].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — There is also agreement on the North American
analogue, though its terms vary. Carlson and Ludlam treat Spain and Mexico as the
two instances of one configuration [SP001, PDF p. 1]. Bennett et al. state the
situation "is similar to the situation in the USA where hot dry air from the
Mexican plateau is found in the Midwestern states above the moist air from the
Gulf of Mexico" [SP026, PDF p. 11]. Lewis and Gray call the analogue "the Mexican
plume" [SP031, PDF p. 3]; Peyraud describes the Great Plains setup as analogous
[SP040, PDF p. 3]; Westermayer et al. link the same layer type to 'Tornado alley'
[SP064, PDF p. 9]; Taszarek et al. locate the American EML source in northern
Mexico, the southwestern United States and the Rocky Mountains [SP081, PDF p. 8].

## 9. Disagreements, unsupported assumptions, and uncertainty

**Direct finding** — The corpus itself records that the pattern resists objective
definition. Wilkinson and Neal state plainly that "it is difficult to define a
Spanish Plume by pressure patterns alone", showing that a documented plume case
(28 June 2011) is diagnosed by ERA5 as pattern 6, an Azores high extension, rather
than as either of the strong-southerly-flow patterns they associate with plumes;
for that reason they decline to name patterns 16 and 22 as Spanish plumes and
call them strong southerly flow instead [SP083, PDF p. 11]. Hayward et al.
reproduce the same difficulty, noting the diagnosis "may demonstrate the
limitations of classifying very variable synoptic conditions into 30 general
patterns" [SP102, PDF p. 17].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — That caution is not carried downstream
consistently. Later work labels pattern 16 outright as "the Spanish Plume weather
pattern" [SP102, PDF p. 16] and tabulates events "under weather pattern 16
(Spanish Plume)" [SP090, PDF p. 19]. Reading the label as an identity converts a
statistical association, which its originators explicitly qualified [SP083,
PDF p. 11], into a definition. The corpus provides no source that validates
pattern 16 as necessary or sufficient for a Spanish plume.

**Direct finding** — Several sources explicitly exclude events from the category,
demonstrating that the concept is used with real, if informal, boundaries. Sibley
and Denning state that the convective activity "was not typical of a classic
'Spanish Plume' event because the thundery cells that brought the heaviest
rainfall formed locally, in association with low-level convergence areas" [SP048,
PDF p. 1]. Grahame and Page report a case where, "in contrast to a Spanish Plume,
a warm airmass was being advected slowly southwards" [SP049, PDF p. 1] and note
"atypical characteristics relative to Spanish Plume cases" [SP049, PDF p. 3].
Webb notes that the 20 July 2021 hailstorms "differed considerably" from the
scenario, with the upper trough further east and storm tracks from
west-northwest [SP096, PDF p. 3]. Webb and Blackshaw describe 2011 Scottish
outbreaks as "not the 'Spanish plume' scenario" [SP036, PDF p. 5]. Scheffknecht
et al. state that their case bears only "superficial similarity" to the setting
[SP058, PDF p. 4].

**Direct finding** — At the other end, some sources apply the label with weak or
absent qualification. Archer and Fowler equate the term with a different
phenomenon entirely — "a tongue of moist air extending from the Tropics — often
called a 'Spanish Plume' or 'Atmospheric River'" [SP067, PDF p. 6]. Lewis et al.
describe plumes as "bringing warm air polewards from the Equator" [SP100,
PDF p. 12]. Brown and Buchanan's account is internally inconsistent: it defines
the plume as "warm, dry air from the Iberian plateau (the Spanish plume) [that]
moves northwards rising over a layer of cool, moist air from the Atlantic", then
describes energy being stored in the lower layer and released when daytime heating
raises the low-level temperature above that of the plume layer [SP070, PDF p. 2],
a description that does not correspond to the CAPE/CIN mechanism given in the
analytical sources [SP026, PDF p. 11; SP031, PDF p. 2].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — The single most consequential unresolved
disagreement is referential, and it is invisible in most of the literature: the
same name denotes the *capping dry elevated layer* in [SP001, PDF p. 1], [SP014,
PDF p. 2], [SP040, PDF p. 2], [SP050, PDF p. 6], [SP062, PDF p. 5], [SP070,
PDF p. 2] and [SP073, PDF p. 15], and the *moist low-level plume beneath the cap*
in [SP031, PDF p. 1], [SP083, PDF p. 11] and [SP102, PDF p. 16]. Only [SP056,
PDF p. 4] flags any ambiguity in the term at all, and it flags a different one
(airstream versus overall pattern). Because the two referents have opposite
moisture and stability properties, statements of the form "the Spanish plume is
warm and moist" and "the Spanish plume is warm and dry" are both well attested in
the corpus and cannot both describe the same object.

**Direct finding** — A claimed trend in event frequency is asserted without
supporting analysis. Sibley states that "after several had occurred between 2004
and 2006, Spanish Plume events have been rare in recent years" [SP034, PDF p. 4],
a claim presented in a four-page magazine case report with no climatology. It sits
uneasily with Lewis and Gray's finding of "no obvious trend in annual frequency"
of UK MCSs across 1981–2008 [SP031, PDF p. 5] and with subsequent corpus sources
documenting classic and modified plume episodes in 2012, 2013 and 2014 [SP066,
PDF pp. 7–8]. Prichard, writing independently, attributes a reduction in British
thundery activity to "a more mobile weather pattern in recent summers" and reports
that in 2011 such developments frequently went from northern France to the Low
Countries instead [SP038, PDF p. 3].

**Direct finding** — The claim that the plume is *the* dominant thunderstorm
mechanism for Britain and Ireland is stated but is qualified within the same
corpus. Wilkinson and Neal write that it "is believed to be the predominant
mechanism which leads to thunderstorms in the summertime over Britain and Ireland"
[SP083, PDF p. 11] — the phrasing itself marks it as belief rather than result.
Holley et al. find the opposite for CAPE generation: while Spanish plume events
"often yield the highest CAPE in Britain, they are also infrequent", and "The
majority of 'land CAPE' is generated as a result of cooling aloft by polar air
masses, coupled with warm LSTs" [SP046, PDF p. 9]. Elsom and Webb's fatality
analysis assigns only 4 of 19 days to plume situations [SP060, PDF p. 12].

**Direct finding** — Several sources qualify the concept's usefulness even while
applying it. Owens asks rhetorically what a user should infer from trough lines
inside warm sectors — "Split front? Spanish Plume? A splash of unexplained rain?"
— in a critique of frontal analysis practice [SP054, PDF p. 3]. Young and
Grahame note that model guidance "had been inconsistent in this case (as in many
Spanish Plume events)" [SP097, PDF p. 6]. Lewis and Gray state that "the
proportion of such events is still uncertain and detailed descriptions of the
alternative synoptic environments are lacking" when motivating their study
[SP031, PDF p. 3].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — Aggregating the above, the corpus supports a
graded reading rather than a binary one. Well-supported: the existence, structure
and capping role of an Iberian/North African EML over western Europe, and its
association with organised severe convection. Moderately supported: the
three-environment taxonomy, which rests on one subjective classification of 25
cases [SP031, PDF pp. 4, 19] and has been applied but not independently re-derived
elsewhere in the corpus [SP042, PDF p. 1; SP066, PDF p. 8; SP090, PDF p. 28].
Weakly supported: any claim that "Spanish plume" designates a well-defined,
reproducibly identifiable class of events. Not established by the provided
sources: an objective, quantitative definition of a Spanish plume event, or a
climatology of Spanish plume events as such (as distinct from climatologies of
MCSs [SP016, PDF p. 7; SP031, PDF p. 5], CAPE days [SP046, PDF p. 9], or weather
patterns [SP083, PDF p. 20]).

## 10. Remaining research gaps

**Direct finding** — Gaps are named explicitly by corpus authors. Dahl and Fischer
state that the dynamical origin of western European warm-season convergence
lines "has barely been addressed in the formal literature despite their ubiquity"
[SP056, PDF p. 1]. Young and Grahame state that overnight plume convection is
triggered above the surface "by processes that were poorly understood and remain
so to this day" [SP097, PDF p. 6]. Lewis and Gray identify the missing proportion
and description of non-classical environments as the knowledge gap their paper
addresses [SP031, PDF p. 3], and call their own categorisation a prerequisite to
future objective analyses [SP031, PDF p. 4]. Young et al. propose that future work
should distinguish "'Spanish Plume' (elevated mixed layer) convection" from other
elevated convection such as that continuing beneath a cold pool aloft, since the
two "exhibit very different vertical profiles of moisture and wind" [SP078,
PDF p. 10].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference** — Six further gaps follow from what the corpus
does not contain. (i) *A definition test*: no source offers reproducible
thresholds by which an event either is or is not a Spanish plume, and the one
attempt to map the concept onto objective circulation classes reports failure on a
benchmark case [SP083, PDF p. 11; SP102, PDF p. 17]. (ii) *Source attribution at
climatological scale*: trajectory evidence for the EML's origin exists for three
individual cases only, and two of those point to North Africa rather than Iberia
[SP061, PDF p. 2; SP076, PDF p. 5; SP084, PDF p. 4], while the relative frequency
of Iberian versus Saharan and Alpine/Turkish sources [SP056, PDF p. 4; SP081,
PDF p. 8] is nowhere quantified. (iii) *Resolution of the referential
inconsistency* between the plume-as-cap and plume-as-moist-inflow usages
documented in §2. (iv) *Discrimination among release mechanisms*: the six
mechanisms in §6 have never been compared within one framework or one dataset.
(v) *Trend detection*: the corpus contains an assertion of declining frequency
[SP034, PDF p. 4], a finding of no MCS trend to 2008 [SP031, PDF p. 5], and an
expectation of intensifying rainfall in such events under warming coupled with
uncertainty about dynamical change [SP079, PDF pp. 21–22], but no trend analysis
of plume events themselves. (vi) *Forecast-skill verification specific to the
pattern*: the corpus reports downstream forecast-impact studies for MCSs in a
modified plume environment [SP071, PDF p. 7; SP072, PDF p. 6] and model-domain
design motivated by plume events [SP059, PDF p. 7], but no verification of
plume-event forecasts as a class.

**Author interpretation** — One further gap is implied rather than stated. Because
the label is applied from the UK to the Alps, Poland and the Mediterranean by
analogy [SP023, PDF p. 1; SP041, PDF p. 11; SP068, PDF p. 12; SP074, PDF p. 15;
SP087, PDF p. 7], and because at least one author has coined a parallel term for a
different source region [SP009, PDF p. 8] while another keeps "Saharan plume" and
"Spanish plume" as separate table entries [SP039, PDF p. 9], the corpus leaves
open whether these are instances of one phenomenon or a family of distinct
EML-capped configurations sharing a borrowed name.

## Corpus coverage and sources that could not be used

**Direct finding** — All 102 PDFs were inspected. Each was opened, its page count
confirmed with `pdfinfo`, and its full text extracted page by page with
`pdftotext` and searched for the term family ("Spanish plume", "Iberian plume",
"elevated mixed layer", "EML") with de-hyphenation across line breaks, so that
every occurrence in the corpus was located and read in context. Extended passages
were then read in full from the sources cited above; 80 of the 102 sources carry
a page-level citation in this synthesis. The remaining 22 were inspected and found
to contribute only citation-level or single-clause passing mentions that duplicate
claims already better evidenced elsewhere (SP007, SP010, SP011, SP012, SP015,
SP017, SP021, SP022, SP025, SP027, SP033, SP037, SP043, SP044, SP051, SP053,
SP055, SP075, SP085, SP092), or no occurrence at all (SP020, SP047).

**Direct finding** — Two sources contain no occurrence of the term family anywhere
in their text and therefore contribute no evidence to this synthesis: SP020
(Melani et al. 2003, plumes on top of deep convective storms — "plume" there
refers to a storm-top feature) and SP047 (Webb 2014, TORRO Thunderstorm Division
review for 2013). A third, SP045 (Mayes and Wheeler 2013), contains a single
occurrence inside a summary table of airmass types [SP045, PDF p. 5] and supports
no substantive claim beyond the observation that continental southeasterly types
"Can be destabilised by Spanish plume events".

**Direct finding** — Two further limitations on use should be recorded. First, six
PDFs are magazine pages whose extracted text includes columns from adjacent,
unrelated articles: SP006 PDF p. 12 carries the opening of SP005 and SP038 PDF p. 1
carries the closing column of SP034, while SP033, SP037, SP091 and SP096 carry
fragments of neighbouring items. No claim above is sourced to such a fragment; in
particular, the frequency claim discussed in §9 is attributed to SP034 PDF p. 4,
where it originates, and not to its duplicate on SP038 PDF p. 1. Second, four
sources reference supporting information that is not present in the corpus (SP078,
SP083, SP092, SP102), and SP081 is Part II of a two-part study whose Part I is
absent; nothing in those absent materials has been used or inferred.

**Direct finding** — The excluded review — Schultz, Young and Kirshbaum (2025),
"The Spanish Plume Elevated Mixed Layer: A Review of Its Use and Misuse within the
Scientific Literature" — is not present in `./sources`, is not listed in
`./corpus_index.csv`, and is not cited in the extracted text of any of the 102
PDFs. Its text, bibliography, supplementary material, dataset, classifications and
results have not been used. Where a question would require evidence outside this
