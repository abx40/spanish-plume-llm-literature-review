# Critical closed-corpus synthesis of the scientific literature concerning the Spanish Plume

## Corpus control, coverage, and evidence conventions

The pre-synthesis audit found 102 PDF files in `./sources`, 102 data rows in `./corpus_index.csv` (plus its header), and no pre-existing files in `./outputs`. All 102 PDFs opened successfully and yielded readable text. The exact title of the excluded Schultz, Young, and Kirshbaum (2025) review was absent from both the index and the PDF full text. Two included papers contain an author named Schultz, but neither is the excluded review. These audit facts are process controls, not scientific evidence.

All 102 PDFs were inspected. Analytical case studies, trajectory studies, process-diagnostic modelling, and climatologies were read most closely and weighted above descriptive case notes and passing uses of the term. Technically unusable sources: **none**. Sources not cited below were inspected but did not add independent Spanish-Plume-specific evidence beyond passing, bibliographic, redundant, or tangential material.

Evidence-status labels are used as follows:

- **Direct finding**: a result produced by the cited study's observations, analysis, trajectories, modelling, or defined sample.
- **Author interpretation**: a causal, classificatory, or provenance claim made by the cited author that is not independently demonstrated by that result alone.
- **Cross-paper synthesis inference**: an inference made here by comparing multiple papers; it is not a direct finding of any one paper.

PDF pages are one-based and refer to pages in the supplied files, not journal pagination.

## 1. Evolution of the term and concept

**Direct finding.** The foundational 1968 analysis did not begin with a named synoptic archetype. It described a physical object: potentially very warm air downwind of an arid plateau, elevated above a cooler, moister layer and acting as a lid. In its European cases, the elevated air was associated with the Spanish tableland, whose summertime afternoon potential temperature was contrasted with lower values over moister France; the paper then referred to the feature as a plume of Spanish air [SP001, PDF p. 1] [SP001, PDF p. 4] [SP001, PDF p. 8]. Salter's contemporaneous event analysis explicitly called the warm 850–700-hPa feature the “Spanish plume,” while treating the high wet-bulb-potential-temperature surface air as a distinct, Mediterranean-derived layer beneath it [SP002, PDF p. 6].

**Author interpretation.** By 1986, Morris presented the “Spanish Plume” as a recurring weather pattern in which thunderstorms develop over Iberia or western France and may spread toward the United Kingdom; the conceptual emphasis extended from the elevated warm layer to the evolution of an upper trough, thermal advection, a thundery low, and a frontal zone [SP003, PDF p. 1] [SP003, PDF p. 2]. Later UK case literature similarly used the expression colloquially for the wider environment of warm/moist low-level advection, an approaching upper trough, and organized convection, rather than solely for the elevated mixed layer [SP004, PDF p. 2] [SP004, PDF p. 3].

**Direct finding.** Lewis and Gray formalized the widened usage by classifying 1998–2008 UK MCS environments into “classical Spanish plume,” “modified Spanish plume,” and a non-Spanish analogue, the “European easterly plume”; their pattern matching used upper-, middle-, and lower-level fields and was explicitly subjective [SP031, PDF p. 1] [SP031, PDF p. 4]. Dahl and Fischer later stated the semantic shift directly: the earlier term denoted the Iberian elevated mixed layer (EML), whereas newer usage denoted the overall synoptic pattern involving it [SP056, PDF p. 4].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference.** “Spanish Plume” therefore has at least two historically layered meanings in this corpus: (1) an elevated, warm, dry airstream or EML, and (2) a larger synoptic-convective configuration containing some combination of that EML, low-level moisture, a cyclone or trough, ascent, and shear [SP001, PDF p. 1] [SP031, PDF p. 1] [SP056, PDF p. 4]. Treating all uses as if they named the same object would erase a documented conceptual change.

## 2. Airstream versus synoptic-pattern usage

**Direct finding.** In airstream usage, the plume is a layer with a thermodynamic identity and a trajectory: deep, dry boundary-layer air formed over elevated arid terrain, subsequently advected above cooler/moister air [SP001, PDF p. 4] [SP026, PDF p. 12] [SP056, PDF p. 4]. This usage permits the plume's western or northern edge to be discussed as a material boundary relative to distinct low-level airstreams [SP001, PDF p. 8] [SP026, PDF p. 13].

**Author interpretation.** In synoptic-pattern usage, “Spanish Plume” denotes a bundle of fields: typically a low west or northwest of the affected region, high pressure farther east, southerly or southwesterly flow, warm advection, an upper trough or cut-off low, and an environment favorable for organized convection [SP003, PDF p. 2] [SP031, PDF p. 1]. The “classical” and “modified” categories distinguish cyclone life-cycle or trough geometry, not different substances in a plume [SP031, PDF p. 1].

**Direct finding.** A 2010–2019 British and Irish lightning analysis found that two mean-sea-level-pressure patterns with strong southerly flow were associated with widespread summer lightning and linked them to Spanish-Plume usage, but it also found that a published 2011 “Spanish Plume” case mapped to a different pressure category. The authors concluded that a Spanish Plume is difficult to define from surface pressure patterns alone [SP083, PDF p. 11] [SP083, PDF p. 12].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference.** The airstream definition is physically narrower and testable with vertical profiles and trajectories; the pattern definition is broader and operationally convenient but classification-dependent. A surface synoptic pattern is neither a sufficient observation of an EML nor proof of its geographical origin [SP031, PDF p. 4] [SP083, PDF p. 11]. A necessary-and-sufficient synoptic definition of “Spanish Plume” is **Not established by the provided sources.**

## 3. Thermodynamic and moisture properties

**Direct finding.** The most consistent thermodynamic profile is a warm, dry, well-mixed elevated layer over a cooler but moister lower layer. In the foundational analysis, dry convection over central Spain produced high potential temperature but relatively low wet-bulb potential temperature, whereas moisture and insolation increased wet-bulb potential temperature in the shallow French layer trapped below [SP001, PDF p. 4] [SP001, PDF p. 7]. The later convection-initiation review expressed the same distinction as high-θ, warm dry Spanish air above lower-θ but higher-θw air in southwest France [SP026, PDF p. 11] [SP026, PDF p. 12].

**Direct finding.** The inversion at the EML base creates convective inhibition (CIN), confines shallow convection, and allows convective available potential energy (CAPE) to accumulate if the lower layer continues warming or moistening [SP001, PDF p. 1] [SP026, PDF p. 11] [SP026, PDF p. 12]. A ten-year WRF climatology found its highest simulated British CAPE during events labelled Spanish Plumes, locally above 3000 J kg−1, but also documented model moisture overestimation and stressed that CAPE is not a direct predictor of thunderstorms [SP046, PDF p. 1] [SP046, PDF p. 5].

**Direct finding.** High CAPE may coexist with either strong or weak CIN depending on the event stage and location. In the 28–29 May 1999 MCS analysis, observed initiation occurred on an elevated plume in high CAPE but outside the high-CIN region, and much of the CAPE area had almost zero CIN [SP027, PDF p. 5] [SP027, PDF p. 6]. In the August 2003 CAPE case, the day with the largest eastward-shifted CAPE produced no further thunderstorms because CIN was high and the short-wave forcing had departed [SP046, PDF p. 5].

**Direct finding.** The lower-layer moisture source is not synonymous with the EML source. Salter traced exceptionally high surface wet-bulb potential temperature over France back to the Mediterranean, beneath warm air over Spain and France [SP002, PDF p. 6]. The Lake Geneva supercell study described warm moist Mediterranean air below a drier, very warm Iberian midlevel layer [SP040, PDF p. 5]. Back trajectories for the 1 July 2015 UK event separated hot, initially dry midlevel air from the Spanish Plateau from shallower moist air that had passed through the Bay of Biscay and, at the lowest level, the southern North Sea [SP061, PDF p. 2].

**Author interpretation.** Some later papers instead call the plume itself “warm and humid” and assign the combined air mass to Iberia [SP035, PDF p. 3]. That wording is compatible with broad synoptic-pattern usage, but it conflicts with the narrower EML concept in which warmth/dryness aloft and moisture below have different histories [SP001, PDF p. 4] [SP026, PDF p. 11].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference.** The stable synthesis is therefore vertical, not geographical: a steep-lapse-rate elevated layer overlies a moisture-bearing lower layer, producing potential or conditional instability that may be capped. The moisture-bearing layer can have Mediterranean, Biscay/Atlantic, North Sea, French continental, or mixed/local contributions in the documented cases [SP002, PDF p. 6] [SP040, PDF p. 5] [SP061, PDF p. 2]. A single, universal moisture source is **Not established by the provided sources.**

## 4. Claimed geographical origin and transport pathways

**Direct finding.** The Spanish tableland is the original European source region proposed for the warm elevated layer: its elevation and aridity support a deeper, hotter dry convective boundary layer than the lower, moister terrain of France [SP001, PDF p. 4]. Isentropic analyses in the foundational cases showed a middle-tropospheric plume extending north from Spain into France and toward the English Channel, while separate upper-tropospheric flow could have Saharan ancestry [SP001, PDF p. 6] [SP001, PDF p. 8].

**Direct finding.** Later analytical work broadened the plausible EML source domain. Van Delden showed warm-plume cases arising not only over Iberia but also over northern Africa and the European continent [SP019, PDF p. 13]. Dahl and Fischer identified Iberia as the main western-European EML source while also naming northern Africa as important; their WRF case had an 850-hPa thermal ridge extending from the southwestern Mediterranean into France [SP056, PDF p. 4] [SP056, PDF p. 6]. A June 2019 central-European severe-weather analysis attributed its EML to the Algerian Sahara and a path across the western Mediterranean and Alps, based on model back trajectories [SP084, PDF p. 4].

**Direct finding.** Event-specific trajectory evidence demonstrates several pathways rather than a single canonical one. For 28 June 2011, NAME III and HYSPLIT back trajectories at 1000, 2000, and 3000 m pointed to Morocco on 23–24 June, with Saharan dust deposition providing an independent qualitative tracer [SP034, PDF p. 2]. For 1 July 2015, 66-h trajectories supported Spanish-Plateau midlevel air reaching northern England through ascent, while lower layers had different paths [SP061, PDF p. 2]. For the July 2019 heat episode, trajectories showed some Algerian air reaching London and northern Europe, but the analysis found adiabatic subsidence and local/continental warming more important overall and described North-African advection as limited [SP076, PDF p. 6].

**Author interpretation.** The commonly claimed corridor is north or northeast from Iberia or northern Africa across Spain, the Bay of Biscay or France, and then toward the UK, Benelux, Germany, Switzerland, or Scandinavia [SP001, PDF p. 8] [SP031, PDF p. 18] [SP061, PDF p. 2] [SP084, PDF p. 4]. Individual papers sometimes infer that corridor from analyzed temperature or equivalent-potential-temperature tongues without parcel trajectories [SP004, PDF p. 11] [SP035, PDF p. 3].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference.** Iberia is a recurrent and physically plausible EML source, and northern Africa is a demonstrated source in some events; neither origin is universal. The lower moist feed frequently follows a different route from the EML [SP019, PDF p. 13] [SP034, PDF p. 2] [SP061, PDF p. 2]. A corpus-wide frequency distribution of source regions and transport pathways, based on consistently calculated parcel histories, is **Not established by the provided sources.**

## 5. Evidence used to support origin claims

**Direct finding.** Four evidence classes recur, with unequal inferential force:

1. **Thermodynamic fingerprints and soundings.** High potential temperature, low moisture, and a deep mixed layer are matched to the climatology of hot elevated terrain [SP001, PDF p. 4] [SP026, PDF p. 12]. This supports an arid-terrain EML interpretation but does not uniquely identify Spain.
2. **Synoptic and isentropic analyses.** Streamlines, limiting streamlines, temperature fields, and confluence zones were used to reconstruct the European cases [SP001, PDF p. 3] [SP001, PDF p. 8]. The foundational analyses assumed near-steady, approximately adiabatic large-scale flow, so their paths are dynamically reasoned reconstructions rather than directly tracked air samples [SP001, PDF p. 3].
3. **Numerical fields and process diagnostics.** ECMWF analyses and frontogenesis diagnostics linked a southern warm tongue to a prefrontal circulation [SP014, PDF p. 17]; WRF simulations and a three-year line/no-line composite linked EML thermal-ridge deformation to convergence-line formation [SP056, PDF p. 9] [SP056, PDF p. 12]. These diagnose dynamics well but do not, by themselves, prove the land-surface history of every parcel.
4. **Lagrangian trajectories and tracers.** Multi-level back trajectories, sometimes combined with dust observations, provide the clearest event-specific provenance evidence [SP034, PDF p. 2] [SP061, PDF p. 2]. Forward and backward trajectories in the 2019 heat study also show why “southerly” does not automatically mean predominantly North-African heat [SP076, PDF p. 6].

**Author interpretation.** Numerous case studies assign Spanish or Iberian origin from a warm tongue, a synoptic resemblance, or inherited terminology without presenting trajectories [SP004, PDF p. 11] [SP035, PDF p. 3] [SP063, PDF p. 6]. Such assignments are hypotheses consistent with the fields, not independent verification of origin.

**Cross-paper synthesis inference.** Repeated assertions of Spanish origin do not become direct evidence through repetition. The strongest evidence supports specific events; extending it to every event labelled “Spanish Plume” is **Not established by the provided sources.**

## 6. Capping, instability, and convection-initiation mechanisms

**Direct finding.** The cap has a dual role. It suppresses premature shallow convection and permits heat and moisture to accumulate below, but a stronger cap also requires greater lifting or erosion before deep convection can start [SP026, PDF p. 11] [SP026, PDF p. 12]. The foundational cases placed release near the edge of the lid, where backed low-level flow emerged from beneath the elevated plume and cold-frontal shear favored storm organization [SP001, PDF p. 1] [SP001, PDF p. 8] [SP026, PDF p. 13].

**Direct finding.** Synoptic ascent can weaken or bypass the cap through several routes: differential thermal advection, approaching upper-level troughs and short waves, an upper cold front/dry intrusion, and frontal ascent [SP005, PDF p. 2] [SP026, PDF p. 11] [SP027, PDF p. 5]. In a diagnosed 1992 squall-line case, cross-frontal circulation generated by frontogenesis placed ascent at the leading edge of the frontal zone and was interpreted as decisive for initiation [SP014, PDF p. 17]. A four-year western-European study likewise found severe storms associated with potential instability and emphasized frontogenesis/cyclogenesis as a major release mechanism north of the Alps and Pyrenees [SP019, PDF p. 13] [SP019, PDF p. 21].

**Direct finding.** Dahl and Fischer added a process link between the EML and a prefrontal convergence line: deformation of the 850-hPa thermal ridge generated quasigeostrophic frontogenesis, an ageostrophic circulation, ascent, and surface convergence, with the signal strongest near the EML's western periphery [SP056, PDF p. 1] [SP056, PDF p. 12]. This partly addresses an earlier cold-front review's criticism that the proposed Spanish-Plume second-front mechanism had not been diagnosed with real data, although the later result still relies on model fields and composites [SP025, PDF p. 9] [SP056, PDF p. 9].

**Direct finding.** Mesoscale and local initiation can depart from that large-scale pathway. Documented triggers or foci include terrain, sea-breeze or land-breeze convergence, prefrontal troughs, weak short waves, upper-level perturbations, convergence lines, and outflow from earlier convection [SP019, PDF p. 21] [SP026, PDF p. 14] [SP063, PDF p. 1]. In the 1 July 2015 elevated-storm case, the exact initiating perturbation could not be resolved despite trajectories, soundings, satellite imagery, and model output [SP061, PDF p. 2].

**Direct finding.** Once convection begins, vertical shear helps separate updrafts and downdrafts and organize long-lived MCSs, supercells, or bow echoes; cold pools and gust fronts can then trigger secondary cells [SP001, PDF p. 1] [SP026, PDF p. 14] [SP063, PDF p. 1]. Dry midlevels and steep lapse rates can also promote evaporatively strengthened downdrafts [SP001, PDF p. 23].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference.** Capping is a frequent precursor and energy-storage mechanism, not a requirement that must remain strong at the observed initiation point. Initiation may occur at the lid edge, after erosion, on an elevated unstable layer, or where focused ascent locally overcomes CIN [SP027, PDF p. 5] [SP027, PDF p. 6] [SP046, PDF p. 5]. No single universal initiation mechanism is established across the corpus.

## 7. Hazards, locations, and event types

**Direct finding.** The most frequently documented outcomes are deep convection and organized convective systems producing intense rain, flash flooding, lightning, hail, and damaging gusts. UK MCS case and climatology studies place many systems over France or the Bay of Biscay before their arrival over Britain, commonly during evening or overnight hours [SP004, PDF p. 1] [SP026, PDF p. 14] [SP031, PDF p. 18]. In the 1981–1997 UK sample, 25 of 32 MCSs were classified as Spanish-Plume-associated, but this is an event classification result rather than proof that Spanish Plumes are necessary for UK MCSs [SP016, PDF p. 7] [SP026, PDF p. 14].

**Direct finding.** Individual severe events show the range of convective hazards. The 28 June 2012 English Midlands event produced flash flooding, tornadoes, and a 110-km supercell hail swath with widespread hail at least 50 mm and some reports of 75–90 mm [SP042, PDF p. 1] [SP042, PDF p. 5]. The 18 July 2005 France–Switzerland supercell produced golf-ball hail, a measured gust near 160 km h−1, two tornadoes, and extensive damage [SP040, PDF p. 1] [SP040, PDF p. 24]. The 14 July 2010 Belgium–Netherlands derecho produced widespread wind damage, two fatalities, and embedded mesovortices/tornadic features [SP035, PDF p. 1] [SP035, PDF p. 17]. The 9 June 2014 Belgium–western Germany bow echo produced gusts near 40 m s−1, hail, casualties, fatalities, and major insured losses [SP063, PDF p. 1].

**Direct finding.** The relevant event types include isolated elevated storms, multicells, squall lines, MCSs/MCCs, supercells, bow echoes, derechos, tornado-bearing storms, and hailstorms [SP026, PDF p. 14] [SP035, PDF p. 1] [SP040, PDF p. 1] [SP063, PDF p. 1]. A French 2000–2022 climatology found 38 warm-season derechos, mostly moving northeast under southwesterly midlevel flow; many 500-hPa patterns resembled a Spanish Plume, but resemblance was not used to demonstrate that all 38 were Spanish-Plume events [SP101, PDF p. 1] [SP101, PDF p. 7].

**Direct finding.** Heat is associated with some broad pattern uses, but its provenance is not simple. The July 2019 analysis concluded that subsidence and continental/local warming dominated much of the western and central European heat, with Spanish-Plume advection boosting the UK episode and only limited transport from North Africa [SP076, PDF p. 6]. Thus extreme heat during a labelled event is not direct evidence that the heat was imported from Africa.

**Direct finding.** Meteotsunamis are a documented secondary coastal hazard of some convective Spanish-Plume cases. A 2017 MCS traversing Spain/Biscay, the English Channel, and the southern North Sea generated a pressure disturbance and meteotsunami through a convective downdraft/gravity-wave mechanism and Proudman resonance [SP086, PDF p. 11] [SP086, PDF p. 12]. However, the larger 2010–2017 northwest-European climatology found most meteotsunamis in winter and only said its summertime quasi-linear composite was broadly consistent with a Spanish-Plume pattern [SP088, PDF p. 1] [SP088, PDF p. 14]. A UK catalogue likewise found a 71% association between summer events and convective weather systems that can occur within Spanish-Plume settings, while documenting a distinct winter pathway involving midlatitude depressions [SP100, PDF p. 13].

**Cross-paper synthesis inference.** The hazard footprint extends from Iberia and France through the UK and southern North Sea into Benelux, Germany, Switzerland, and adjacent central Europe, but the best-documented impacts are concentrated where the corpus itself is concentrated [SP031, PDF p. 18] [SP040, PDF p. 24] [SP063, PDF p. 1]. Neither severe convection nor any individual hazard is diagnostic of a Spanish Plume: alternative European easterly-plume, slack-low, frontal, blocked, polar-maritime, and local-trigger environments also produce thunderstorms or related hazards [SP031, PDF p. 1] [SP083, PDF p. 12] [SP088, PDF p. 14].

## 8. Areas of agreement

**Cross-paper synthesis inference.** The strongest cross-paper agreement concerns ingredients rather than terminology or origin:

- A warm-season EML can overrun a moisture-bearing lower layer, producing steep lapse rates, potential/conditional instability, and often CIN [SP001, PDF p. 1] [SP026, PDF p. 11] [SP056, PDF p. 4].
- The lid can allow energy to accumulate, but deep convection requires sufficiently focused lift or access to a weak/absent portion of the cap [SP026, PDF p. 11] [SP027, PDF p. 5] [SP046, PDF p. 5].
- Troughs, fronts, short waves, frontogenesis, or convergence commonly supply large-scale to mesoscale ascent, while local boundaries and orography can determine the exact initiation point [SP014, PDF p. 17] [SP019, PDF p. 21] [SP026, PDF p. 14].
- Vertical shear is important for organization and longevity after initiation [SP001, PDF p. 1] [SP026, PDF p. 14] [SP063, PDF p. 1].
- Iberia is a frequent plausible EML source and northern Africa is a verified source in some events, but lower-layer moisture often has a different source [SP019, PDF p. 13] [SP034, PDF p. 2] [SP061, PDF p. 2].

## 9. Disagreements, unsupported assumptions, and uncertainty

**Cross-paper synthesis inference — object named by the term.** The largest disagreement is semantic: some papers mean the EML, others the combined low-level warm/moist tongue, and others an entire cyclone–trough–convection pattern [SP001, PDF p. 4] [SP031, PDF p. 1] [SP056, PDF p. 4]. This makes event counts and causal claims non-comparable unless the operational definition is stated.

**Cross-paper synthesis inference — moisture and origin.** The original layer-separated model conflicts with later wording that assigns a warm, humid plume wholesale to Iberia [SP002, PDF p. 6] [SP035, PDF p. 3]. Trajectory studies show Spanish, Moroccan, Algerian, Biscay, North Sea, and mixed paths, and one heat study found that much of the warming was produced by subsidence rather than subtropical advection [SP034, PDF p. 2] [SP061, PDF p. 2] [SP076, PDF p. 6]. A universal Iberian or North-African origin, particularly for moisture, is **Not established by the provided sources.**

**Cross-paper synthesis inference — trigger.** Lid-edge escape, frontogenesis, upper-cold-front ascent, terrain, sea breezes, convergence lines, short waves, and prior-storm outflow all have support in particular studies [SP001, PDF p. 8] [SP014, PDF p. 17] [SP019, PDF p. 21] [SP026, PDF p. 14] [SP063, PDF p. 1]. Recasting any one as the defining or universal trigger is unsupported.

**Cross-paper synthesis inference — cap strength.** The literature sometimes treats a strong cap as central, yet observed initiation can occur where CIN has already become negligible [SP026, PDF p. 11] [SP027, PDF p. 6]. Conversely, large CAPE can remain unused when CIN persists and forcing is absent [SP046, PDF p. 5]. Neither high CAPE nor a visually compelling plume is sufficient for storms.

**Direct finding — classification uncertainty.** The Lewis–Gray MCS taxonomy was subjective and based partly on pattern matching and sounding indications of origin [SP031, PDF p. 4]. The pressure-pattern/lightning study showed that a named Spanish-Plume event can fall outside the expected pressure categories and that mean pressure patterns conceal substantial within-category variability [SP083, PDF p. 11] [SP083, PDF p. 12]. Therefore, pattern resemblance should not be counted as independent thermodynamic or trajectory confirmation.

**Cross-paper synthesis inference — hazard attribution.** Case studies demonstrate possibility, not hazard probability. The corpus contains striking hail, tornado, derecho, flood, wind, heat, and meteotsunami cases, but it lacks a consistently defined denominator of all Spanish-Plume environments, including null events [SP001, PDF p. 23] [SP046, PDF p. 5] [SP088, PDF p. 14]. Absolute hazard rates conditional on a rigorously defined Spanish Plume are **Not established by the provided sources.**

## 10. Remaining research gaps

**Cross-paper synthesis inference.** The following gaps remain after weighting the analytical studies above the passing mentions:

1. **Operational definition.** A reproducible definition should specify whether the target is an EML, a multi-airstream vertical structure, or a synoptic pattern, and should define measurable thresholds for layer depth, lapse rate, moisture contrast, CIN, and provenance [SP031, PDF p. 4] [SP056, PDF p. 4] [SP083, PDF p. 11]. Such a consensus definition is **Not established by the provided sources.**
2. **Source-attribution climatology.** Event-by-event, multi-level trajectories and tracer or moisture-budget diagnostics are needed to quantify Iberian, Saharan, Mediterranean, Atlantic/Biscay, continental, and local contributions separately [SP034, PDF p. 2] [SP061, PDF p. 2] [SP076, PDF p. 6].
3. **EML formation and modification.** Observations should test how soil moisture, sensible heat flux, plateau residence time, diabatic mixing, subsidence, and ascent transform the layer between source and receptor [SP001, PDF p. 4] [SP026, PDF p. 12].
4. **Initiation predictability.** High-resolution observations and ensembles are needed to distinguish cap erosion from cap-edge escape and to resolve weak short waves, gravity waves, boundaries, and prior-convection outflows; even instrument-rich case studies can leave the initiating perturbation uncertain [SP026, PDF p. 17] [SP061, PDF p. 2].
5. **Null-event and denominator sampling.** Studies should sample both convective and non-convective plume environments so that necessary, sufficient, and probabilistic roles of CAPE, CIN, lift, and shear can be separated [SP001, PDF p. 23] [SP046, PDF p. 5].
6. **Objective hazard climatology.** Comparable catalogues are needed for MCSs, hail, tornadoes, severe wind, flooding, and heat using the same plume definition and accounting for reporting changes [SP031, PDF p. 4] [SP101, PDF p. 7].
7. **Coastal coupling.** The convective-pressure-wave pathway to meteotsunamis needs higher-frequency tide-gauge observations, storm-resolving atmospheric modelling, and explicit comparison with the more common winter mechanisms [SP086, PDF p. 12] [SP088, PDF p. 14] [SP100, PDF p. 13].
8. **Climate-change attribution.** The derecho literature reports recent CAPE changes but unresolved contributions from circulation variability and anthropogenic forcing; a Spanish-Plume-specific trend cannot be inferred without a stable definition and source-aware historical classification [SP101, PDF p. 1]. It is **Not established by the provided sources.**
